⚖️Your idea, checked against the numbers

You proposed: four posts only, 4-inch pipe, pulled in from the edges and close together. Two of those three are right. One needs a number put on it.

✔ Four posts — correct. Each post only has to carry about 550 kg of service load. A 100 mm concrete column filled to a 17 MPa mix has roughly 110 kN (11 tonnes) of pure axial capacity. You are using about 5% of it. Six posts, as suggested earlier, buys you nothing here and costs two more footings. Four is the right call.

✔ Inset from the edge — correct, and it makes the beams smaller. With posts at the edge, the whole 3 m is one span. Pull them in and the overhangs push back against the middle. Balancing the two gives the exact optimum: inset = deck ÷ 4.828 = 0.62 m, which is why this design lands on a clean 0.60 m inset / 1.80 m post grid.

✘ "Much closer together" — this is the part to stop. Tighter clustering is not free. The platform's own weight is only about 200 kg, so the restoring moment is small. At a 1.8 m grid, three adults (240 kg) standing on one edge produce 144 kg·m of tipping against 180 kg·m of restoring — already only 1.25× margin. Squeeze the grid to 1.0 m and it becomes 240 kg·m against 100 kg·m: it tips. Below 1.5 m the deck also starts feeling springy when you walk to the edge.

The fix that makes all of it work: tie the four footings together underground with a grade beam — a 200 × 300 mm concrete strap running post-to-post below grade, poured in the same session as the footings. That turns four separate footings into one rigid 1.8 × 1.8 m base weighing about 2 tonnes. Tipping margin goes from 1.25× to about 14×, because the two tonnes of buried concrete now count as restoring weight instead of four separate lumps that can each rotate on their own. It costs roughly ₱4,000 in concrete and steel and it is the single highest-value line item in this whole build.

🧭How the structure works

Four jobs, four different materials — each doing the one thing it is cheap at.

Grade beam ties 4 footings into one base→ Concrete columns take the weight→ X-bracing takes the sway→ Steel beams span & cantilever→ Wood deck on top

The five things that decide whether this works

① The PVC is a mould, not a post. The 4" pipe is formwork plus a permanent rain jacket. The concrete and the rebar cage inside it are the structure. Never size the column off the pipe's strength.
② Bracing is not optional at this slenderness. Unbraced, a 100 mm column 2.44 m tall has a slenderness ratio around 195 — far past any usable limit. X-brace three faces and it drops to about 98 and the columns go back to just carrying weight.
③ The grade beam is the whole ballgame. Inset posts mean a narrow base, and the deck only weighs ~200 kg — on its own that is a tippy table. Strapping the four footings into one block is what lets their two tonnes count as ballast, and what fixes the column bases so they are not free-headed cantilevers. Do not skip it to save ₱4,000.
④ Small aggregate or the pour fails. Inside a 100 mm pipe with four 10 mm bars and ties, the clear gaps are ~37 mm. Use 3/8" washed gravel and a wet, flowable 1:2:4 mix, rodded in 300 mm lifts. Standard 3/4" gravel will bridge and leave voids you cannot see.
⑤ The tent comes down before a typhoon. Always. Batangas sits in NSCP Zone II (200 kph basic wind). With the tent and tarp up, a design-level storm puts roughly 3,400 kg·m of overturning against the base's ~2,000 kg·m. Bare deck, tent and tarp and palupo removed, it is about 500 against 2,000 — fine. This is an operating rule, not a nice-to-have.
⑥ Coco lumber must be treated. Untreated coco lumber outdoors in Batangas is bukbok food inside two or three years. Two coats of wood preservative on every face before assembly, including the cut ends.

🛒The choices you actually have to make

Structural material (pipe, rebar, cement, tubular steel, lumber) comes from a local hardware and lumberyard — not online. Only the consumables below are worth buying on Shopee. Prices are indicative PH ranges and move with stock and the peso.

Decision 1 · Deck surface

Plank deck, not plywood

2 × 4 treated planks with 5 mm gaps drain and air-dry, and last 10 years-plus outdoors. Marine plywood is ₱1,000 cheaper and smoother but delaminates at the edges in a few PH rainy seasons. Best of both: plank deck outdoors + a light plywood or rubber mat you lay on top only when the tent is pitched, stored indoors the rest of the time.

₱4,350 – ₱6,500 planks · ₱3,300 – ₱4,300 ply
Decision 2 · Stair

Fixed 45° stair, lift-out bottom step

8 ft of rise at 45° needs 2.44 m of run and a 3.45 m stringer — too long and heavy to fold neatly under a 3 m deck. Make it fixed and take the bottom 600 mm out instead: nothing climbs up, and it costs ₱600 in pins. A hinged rope-and-pulley "drawbridge" stair works and looks great but adds ₱1,500–2,800 and something that can seize.

₱5,750 – ₱8,550 fixed · +₱1,500 – ₱2,800 hinged
Decision 3 · Rust protection

Red oxide + enamel, re-coat every 3 years

Batangas is coastal — salt air eats untreated mild steel. Two coats of red oxide primer plus two of enamel costs ₱1,300–2,000 and buys 3 years before touch-up. Hot-dip galvanising the whole frame is ₱6,000–10,000 and lasts 15+ years with no maintenance. If the platform is within a kilometre of the shore, galvanising is the honest better buy.

₱1,300 – ₱2,000 paint · ₱6,000 – ₱10,000 galvanised

Decision 4 · Tarp & palupo

One 4 × 5 m heavy trapal, guyed both ways

The palupo is a 50 × 50 × 2.0 steel tube, 2.2 m tall, that drops into a welded sleeve at the middle of one rim beam — lift it straight out when you are done. Run two guy lines from its top back to the opposite deck corners so it cannot bend when the tarp catches wind. 4 × 5 m gives you an overhang past the deck edge so the rain lands off the platform.

Two gallons, two coats, before assembly

This is the difference between a deck that lasts 10 years and one that is powder in three. Coat every face and every cut end before the wood goes up — once it is screwed down you can never reach the contact faces, and that is exactly where the bukbok starts.

Decision 6 · Tent tie-downs

7 eye bolts through the rim beam

The thing nobody plans for: you cannot drive a tent peg into a deck. Bolt seven 10 mm eye bolts through the perimeter rim — four corners, two side midpoints, one at the front — and the tent guys clip straight onto them. ₱400 of hardware that decides whether the tent stays put in a gust.

🔧Step-by-step

Roughly 10–14 working days with three people, assuming a week of dry weather for the concrete.

1▸ Set out and dig 1–2 days
  1. Mark the 3.0 × 3.0 m deck outline with batter boards and string. Check square by measuring the diagonals — both must read 4,243 mm. Do not proceed until they match within 5 mm.
  2. Mark the four post centres 600 mm in from each edge, giving a 1,800 × 1,800 mm square. Check those diagonals too: 2,546 mm.
  3. Dig four pits 600 × 600 mm, 900 mm deep. Connect them with a trench 200 mm wide × 300 mm deep running post-to-post — this is the grade beam and it must be continuous around all four sides.
  4. If you hit soft, wet or filled soil, stop and go deeper until you reach firm ground, or widen the footings to 750 mm square. Soil is the one thing this drawing cannot know for you.
  5. Compact the pit bottoms and lay 50 mm of gravel in each.
2▸ Cages, forms and the one big pour 2 days + 7 days curing
  1. Bend the footing mats: five 10 mm bars each way per footing, 550 mm long, wired at every crossing.
  2. Build the four column cages: four 10 mm bars, 3.1 m long each (2.44 m column + 400 mm into the footing + 300 mm projecting above for the cap plate), with 6 mm circular ties at 150 mm centres. Wire every tie.
  3. Thread the 4" PVC over each cage and cut it to length so the top finishes exactly at deck-underside level. Plumb each pipe two ways and brace it with scrap lumber — once concrete is in, a leaning post stays leaning.
  4. Lay the two continuous 10 mm bars in the grade beam trench, lapping them 400 mm into each footing cage.
  5. Mix Class A 1:2:4 with 3/8" washed gravel and a wet, flowable slump. Pour footings, grade beam and columns in one session — a cold joint at the base of a column is the exact place you do not want one.
  6. Rod the column concrete in 300 mm lifts with a length of 10 mm bar. Tap the PVC with a rubber mallet as you go to bring air out.
  7. Set the 12 mm anchor bolts in the wet column tops, and leave the four projecting rebar tails standing. Cure wet for 7 days before any load goes on.
3▸ Fabricate the steel on the ground 2–3 days
  1. Cut two main beams at 3,000 mm from the 50 × 75 × 2.0 tube. Cut the perimeter rim: four pieces of 50 × 50 × 2.0 at 3,000 mm, mitred at the corners.
  2. Weld the rim into a 3.0 × 3.0 m square frame on flat ground. Check the diagonals again at 4,243 mm before the welds cool.
  3. Weld the two main beams across it at 600 mm in from each edge, flush with the rim's underside.
  4. Add the two 600 mm stub ties: one from the rim to the near beam at the front midpoint (for the palupo), one at the rear midpoint (for the stair).
  5. Weld the palupo sleeve — a 300 mm length of 65 × 65 tube — vertically at the front stub junction, and drill a drain hole in its bottom so rainwater does not sit inside.
  6. Weld the four 150 × 150 × 6 mm cap plates to the beam undersides, directly over where the posts will be.
  7. Prime and paint everything now, while it is on the ground and reachable. Two coats red oxide, two coats enamel.
4▸ Lift, seat and brace 1 day
  1. Four people lift the frame onto the four columns. It weighs roughly 100 kg — do not do this with two.
  2. Drop the cap plates over the anchor bolts, level the frame both ways with steel shims, then tighten.
  3. Bend the four projecting column bars 90° and weld them to the underside of each cap plate. This is the connection that makes it one structure instead of a table standing on four sticks.
  4. Cut and fit the X-bracing from 25 × 25 × 1.5 tube: two diagonals per face, on three faces, leaving the stair face open. Each diagonal is about 2,910 mm.
  5. Weld the brace ends to the cap plates at the top and to plates cast into the pedestals at the bottom. Touch up the paint on every weld.
  6. Push hard on a corner at deck height. It should feel dead solid. If there is any perceptible sway, add the fourth face and live with the stair passing through it.
5▸ Deck it 2 days
  1. Coat every piece of lumber on all faces and both cut ends with preservative, two coats, and let it dry. Do this before anything is fixed.
  2. Lay eight 2 × 4 joists across the two main beams at 400 mm centres, running the 3 m direction, with 600 mm overhanging past each beam. Screw through into the steel with self-drilling screws, or clip them with steel angles.
  3. Lay the 2 × 4 deck planks across the joists with a consistent 5 mm gap — use a screw shank as a spacer. Two screws per plank per joist.
  4. Give the deck a 1% fall to one side so water always has somewhere to go.
  5. Fix the 2 × 2 toe-kerb on edge around the whole perimeter. It is 50 mm tall, nearly invisible, and it is what stops a mattress or a bag sliding off an unrailed 8 ft deck.
  6. Bolt the seven 10 mm eye bolts through the rim beam: four corners, two side midpoints, one at the front.
6▸ Stair, palupo, done 2 days
  1. Pour a 400 × 400 × 200 mm concrete pad where the stair lands, 2,440 mm out from the deck edge. Let it cure before loading.
  2. Cut two 3,450 mm stringers from 50 × 75 tube. Mark nine tread positions: 271 mm rise, 271 mm going — at 45° those are the same number, which makes the setting-out easy.
  3. Weld tread angles, bolt on 2 × 8 treads, and weld a 25 × 25 handrail at 900 mm above the nosing line on at least one side.
  4. Bolt the stringer tops to the rear stub tie and rim beam. Add two knee braces from the rim back to the nearest two columns — the stair's load must reach a post, not hang off the decking.
  5. Make the bottom 600 mm a separate section joined with two drop-in pins, so you can lift it out and nothing climbs up while you are away.
  6. Cut the palupo from 50 × 50 tube, 2,600 mm long — 2,200 mm above the deck, 400 mm down inside the sleeve. Weld an eye at the top and 300 mm down. Drop it in; it should be a snug slide fit, not hammered.
  7. Rig the tarp: ridge line from the palupo eye, two guys from the top back to the opposite deck corners, and the outer corners staked to the ground clear of the deck.

⚠️Safety & pitfalls

  • Have a local structural engineer or an experienced builder look at this before you pour. The numbers here are worked from standard loads and published Philippine material properties, and they are shown so you can check them — but nobody has seen your soil, and soil decides footings. An hour of a local engineer's time is the cheapest insurance in this project.
  • Tent and tarp come down before every typhoon — no exceptions. Batangas is NSCP Zone II, 200 kph basic wind. With the tent up, a design storm generates roughly 3,400 kg·m of overturning against about 2,000 kg·m of resistance. Bare, it is around 500 against 2,000 and it rides out fine. Pull the palupo, strip the tarp, drop the tent.
  • Do not skip the grade beam or the bracing to save money. Without the grade beam the four footings act separately, so only the deck's own ~200 kg resists: three people on one edge sits at a 1.25× margin. Tie them and it is about 14×. Without the bracing, the columns are effectively unrestrained flagpoles at a slenderness ratio near 195 — far outside any code limit. These two items are ~₱6,000 of a ₱75,000 build and they carry the whole safety case.
  • You removed the railing — so respect the edge. 8 ft onto hard ground breaks bones. The 50 mm toe-kerb stops things sliding off but it will not stop a person. No sleeping with a head at the open edge, no alcohol on the deck at night, and think hard before letting small children up there unsupervised. If you ever change your mind, a 900 mm rail on three sides is about ₱4,500 in 25 × 25 tube and mesh.
  • Do not stand on the deck until the concrete has cured 7 days, and do not load it fully for 28. Concrete reaches only about two-thirds of its strength in the first week.
  • Use 3/8" gravel in the columns. With four 10 mm bars and ties inside a 100 mm pipe, standard 3/4" gravel bridges across the gaps and leaves voids you cannot see and cannot fix. This is the single most common way a nice-looking concrete-filled post turns out hollow.
  • Brace the PVC pipes plumb before pouring and check them twice. A post that sets 20 mm out of plumb over 2.44 m will fight you at every later stage and there is no correcting it afterwards.
  • Coastal salt air. Inspect the welds and touch up the paint every year, and re-coat fully every 3 years. If you skip it, the bracing connections rust first — and those are the ones holding the sway.
