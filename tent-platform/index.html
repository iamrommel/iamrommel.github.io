💸What ₱10,000 actually buys — the honest arithmetic

Your sketch specified 4" PVC + concrete columns. I priced them. They alone eat the entire budget, so something had to give — and it is the columns, not the platform.

✘ The 4" PVC + concrete columns do not fit. Four 3 m lengths of 4" PVC (₱2,400–5,200) plus 10 bags of cement, 8 lengths of 10 mm rebar, sand, gravel and tie wire comes to ₱8,800–14,000 for the four posts by themselves. That leaves ₱1,200 or less for a 9 m² deck 8 ft in the air. It cannot be done.

✔ Coco lumber posts do fit — and they are structurally fine here. A 4" × 4" coco post carries roughly 5 tonnes at this height once knee-braced; each post only sees about 550 kg. The concrete was never needed for strength — it was for permanence. Going to timber costs you longevity (10–15 years instead of 30+), not safety.

Where the ₱9,960 goes. Deck area is the cost driver, not height — decking plus joists is 40% of the budget. Dropping from 8 ft to 6 ft saves about ₱250. Dropping from 3.0 m to 2.4 m square saves about ₱1,900.

So ₱10,000 buys the platform, and only the platform: 4 posts, 2 beams, joists, deck, knee braces, concrete pad footings, preservative. No stair, no tarp, no ridge beam. Two ways to get those: build the 3 × 3 m now and add them next payday (see Phases), or build 2.4 × 2.4 m — that comes in at ₱8,030 and leaves ₱1,970, enough for the ridge beam and tarp inside the same ₱10k.

🧭How the structure works

Everything in your sketch is kept: 4 inset posts, ridge beam across the centre, no railing, usable silong. Only the post material changed.

Concrete pad footings→ 4 coco posts, inset 500 mm→ Knee braces kill the sway→ 2 beams cantilever the deck→ Ridge beam + tarp on top

The six things that decide whether this works

① Inset posts have an exact sweet spot. Pull them in and the overhangs push back against the middle of the beam. The two balance at inset = deck ÷ 4.828 = 620 mm. Your sketch says 2.0–2.2 m spacing, so I used 500 mm inset / 2,000 mm grid — very slightly heavier beams, but a wider, steadier base and a far more usable silong. Good call on your part.
② Knee braces instead of X-bracing — because you want the silong. Full-height X-bracing would be stiffer, but it walls in the space underneath. Eight 900 mm knee braces pointing inward do the job instead: they cut the post's effective length from 2.44 m to 1.54 m, taking slenderness from le/d 49 — right on the timber limit of 50 — down to 31. The silong perimeter stays completely clear.
③ Posts must never touch soil. Buried coco lumber is gone in two years. Every post sits on a concrete pedestal 150 mm above grade, located by a rebar dowel, with bitumen between wood and concrete. This one detail is most of the difference between a 3-year deck and a 15-year one.
④ The tent and tarp come down before a typhoon. Batangas is NSCP Zone II, 200 kph. Bare, the platform sits at about a 2.2× margin against overturning — fine. With the tarp up it becomes a sail and that margin is gone. Pull the ridge beam out of its sockets, strip the tarp, drop the tent.
⑤ Two coats of preservative, before assembly. ₱1,100 of the budget, 11%, and it is not optional in Batangas. Every face and every cut end, while the pieces are still loose — once it is nailed down you can never reach the contact faces, and that is exactly where bukbok starts.
⑥ You cannot peg a tent into a deck. Drill through the rim joist and fit rope loops or cheap eye bolts at the corners and side midpoints so the tent guys have something to clip to. Costs almost nothing and decides whether the tent stays put in a gust.

🔧Step-by-step — Phase 1

About 6–8 weekends solo, or 3 long days with two friends. Needs one dry week for the footings.

1▸ Set out and dig 1 day
  1. Mark the 3.0 × 3.0 m deck outline with string and batter boards. Check square by the diagonals — both must read 4,243 mm. Do not move on until they match within 5 mm.
  2. Mark the four post centres 500 mm in from each edge: a 2,000 × 2,000 mm square. Its diagonals should read 2,828 mm.
  3. Dig four pits 500 × 500 mm, 450 mm deep. Compact the bottoms and put 50 mm of gravel in each.
  4. If the ground is soft, wet or filled, go deeper until it is firm, or widen the pads to 600 mm square. Nothing in this drawing knows your soil — you do.
2▸ Pour the pads and pedestals 1 day + 7 days curing
  1. Box a 500 × 500 × 300 mm pad in each pit, with a 200 × 200 mm pedestal on top rising 150 mm above grade.
  2. Stand a 10 mm rebar dowel in the centre of each, projecting 100 mm above the pedestal. This locates the post and holds it down against uplift.
  3. Mix 1:2:4 and pour all four in one session. Check that all four pedestal tops are level with each other with a hose level or a straight board — get this right now and the deck is level for free later.
  4. Cure wet for 7 days. Use the time to cut and treat lumber.
3▸ Cut and treat everything 2 days incl. drying
  1. Cut posts to 2,290 mm (deck top 2,440 − deck build-up 150). Cut beams and joists to 3,000 mm, knee braces to 1,273 mm with 45° ends.
  2. Drill a 12 mm hole 120 mm deep up the centre of each post bottom for the pedestal dowel.
  3. Two coats of preservative on every face and every cut end, then let it dry. This is the step people skip and regret. Do it while the pieces are loose on sawhorses.
4▸ Stand the posts, hang the beams 1 day
  1. Drop each post over its dowel with a scrap of bitumen membrane or rubber between wood and concrete. Plumb it two ways and brace it temporarily with scrap.
  2. Nail up each beam as two 2 × 6 plies with 3" nails staggered at 300 mm, then lift it onto the post tops. Two people minimum.
  3. Fix each beam to its posts with two M12 bolts through the post — not nails. Nails in shear at this joint will loosen and let the deck rock.
  4. Fit all eight knee braces, 900 mm each way, pointing inward, two bolts per end. Then take the temporary bracing off.
  5. Push hard on a post at head height. It should feel dead. If there is any give, check the brace bolts before going further.
5▸ Joists and decking 2 days
  1. Lay the eight 2 × 4 joists across the beams at 400 mm centres, 500 mm overhanging past each beam. Toe-nail them, then add a rim joist right round to lock the ends and stiffen the cantilever edge.
  2. Lay the 1 × 8 deck boards with a consistent 5 mm gap — a nail shank makes a good spacer. Two nails per board per joist.
  3. Give the deck about a 1% fall to one side so water always has somewhere to go.
  4. Rip offcuts into a 50 mm toe-kerb and run it right round the perimeter. Nearly invisible, and with no railing it is what stops a mattress or a bag sliding off.
  5. Drill through the rim at the four corners, the two side midpoints and the front, and fit rope loops or eye bolts for the tent guys.
  6. Mark the two ridge socket positions at the midpoints of two opposite edges now, even if Phase 3 is months away — it saves cutting into finished decking later.

⚠️Safety & pitfalls

  • Tent, tarp and ridge beam come down before every typhoon. Batangas is NSCP Zone II, 200 kph basic wind. Bare, the platform sits at roughly a 2.2× margin against overturning. With the tarp up it is a sail and that margin disappears. The ridge beam lifts out of its sockets in five minutes — that is the whole point of the socket design.
  • Never bury the posts. Coco lumber in contact with soil is gone in two to three years. Pedestal, 150 mm of air, bitumen pad. If you take one detail from this page, take that one.
  • Bolts at the post-to-beam and knee-brace joints, not nails. Nails in shear work loose under repeated load, and "the deck wobbles a bit" is how this fails slowly. Eight M12 bolts is ₱360 of the budget.
  • You removed the railing — so respect the edge. 8 ft onto hard ground breaks bones. The toe-kerb stops things sliding off but will not stop a person. No sleeping with your head at the open edge, and think hard before small children go up unsupervised. A 900 mm rail on three sides in coco lumber is about ₱1,200 if you change your mind.
  • Do not stand on the pedestals for 7 days and do not load the deck fully for 28. Concrete has only about two-thirds of its strength after the first week.
  • Check the whole thing once a year. Tighten every bolt, look for soft spots low on the posts and at the beam ends, and re-coat the preservative every 2–3 years. Timber warns you before it fails, but only if you look.
  • Coco lumber quality varies a lot. Buy at the yard in person and reject pieces with big through-knots, heavy twist, or a soft punky centre — especially the four posts and two beams. Those six pieces carry everything.
  • This is a DIY structure, not an engineered one. The numbers here use standard residential loads and published coconut-lumber values, and they are shown so they can be checked. Nobody has seen your soil. If you can get a local builder to look at the footings before you pour, do.
